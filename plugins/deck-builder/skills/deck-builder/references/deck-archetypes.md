# Deck archetypes

Choose one deck archetype before planning individual slides. Treat the archetype as a higher-order constraint than visual complexity.

Use visual ambition to decide how polished or media-rich the deck should feel. Use the archetype to decide how the deck stays coherent.

## Core rule

- Pick one primary archetype for the whole deck.
- Let that archetype define the recurring frame, cover logic, section rhythm, and allowable variation.
- Richer media is allowed, but it should intensify the chosen system rather than replace it.

## 1) Case-study system

Use for multi-customer stories, proof libraries, sales examples, vertical case studies, or repeated portfolio pages.

- Default structure: one recurring page grammar reused across most or all body slides.
- Best when the same categories repeat across slides such as customer profile, process flow, problem, why the solution won, and target outcomes.
- Keep the grid, headline position, proof modules, and footer structure consistent from slide to slide.
- Allow variation in the evidence, mini-diagram, callout metrics, and accent emphasis, not in the entire layout language.
- Cover should feel credibility-first and related to the body system, not like a separate experimental poster.

If the user points at an existing case-study deck as the model, choose this archetype and keep repetition high.

## 2) Executive narrative

Use for strategic story decks, executive briefings, keynote-style presentations, investor or leadership discussions, and 5 to 12 slide persuasive narratives.

- Default structure: a strong opener, a small set of recurring body patterns, and clear section rhythm.
- Use 2 to 4 signature slide types and repeat them intentionally.
- Allow one or two standout slides, but keep the typography, spacing, and composition family consistent.
- Cover can be more expressive than a case-study deck, but it should still preview the visual language used later in the deck.

This is the best default archetype when the user wants a premium, flexible deck without a rigid repeated frame.

## 3) Product proof

Use for demo stories, workflow walkthroughs, before-and-after product value, solution overviews, or screenshot-led decks.

- Default structure: evidence-first slides anchored by screenshots, concise callouts, or interface-led sequences.
- Keep screenshot framing, annotation style, callout chips, and caption treatment consistent across the deck.
- Use diagrams only when they clarify the workflow around the proof.
- Cover should hint at product evidence rather than inventing a purely decorative hero.

This archetype is often better than rebuilding UI concepts from generic shapes.

## 4) Platform architecture

Use for technical solution storytelling, orchestration models, capability stacks, integration maps, and operating models.

- Default structure: repeatable node styles, layer treatments, connector rules, legends, and foundation bars.
- Keep the visual grammar for systems, agents, robots, people, and governance elements consistent across slides.
- Allow different diagrams, but keep their parts from feeling like they belong to different decks.
- Cover should introduce the architecture language, not just a detached illustration.

This is the right archetype when the deck needs richer diagrams without losing system discipline.

## Archetype versus complexity

- `minimal`: keep the archetype clear with simple placeholders, charts, and restrained diagrams.
- `standard`: use the archetype plus one strong visual idea per slide.
- `rich`: add better hierarchy, vector systems, screenshots, or image-led moments while keeping the same deck grammar.
- `showpiece`: increase polish and ambition, but do not let every slide become a different design experiment.

## Anti-patterns

- Do not mix multiple archetypes casually in a short deck.
- Do not let the cover introduce a totally different graphic language from the body slides.
- Do not use "rich" as permission to vary the layout on every slide.
- Do not turn a case-study system deck into a series of unrelated bespoke slides.
