# Voice and register

## Build a small voice model

Use representative samples from the same author and, when possible, the same channel. Infer tendencies across samples:

- sentence length and variation;
- paragraph density;
- common words and tolerated jargon;
- directness, warmth, skepticism, humor, and emotional range;
- first- and second-person use;
- punctuation, fragments, contractions, and parenthetical asides;
- how the writer opens, transitions, qualifies claims, and ends.

Prefer stable tendencies over a memorable one-off line. Match the author's range, not a caricature. Never reuse a distinctive phrase from an unrelated sample as if it belonged in the new subject.

If no sample exists, infer voice conservatively from the draft. Preserve unusual specifics, mixed feelings, and defensible quirks. Do not manufacture typos, slang, randomness, or false autobiography to seem human.

## Format for the reader

Treat formatting preferences as conditional choices, not an instruction to decorate every
piece. Preserve an explicit plain-text, paragraph-only or spoken format.

- **Selective bold:** emphasize a decision, action, key distinction or short lead-in that
  helps scanning. Leave most text unbolded; do not highlight every sentence or add a
  bold label to every bullet by habit. In decision briefs and action proposals, read
  the headings and bold lead-ins alone: they should state the finding or action, not
  merely announce a topic such as "Coverage issues." The following text supplies the
  evidence, reason or necessary detail.
- **Sub-bullets:** use them for evidence, examples, caveats or next steps that belong to
  one parent point. Keep independent points at the same level. Prefer one useful level
  of nesting; deeper nesting needs a real hierarchy and a channel that renders it well.
- **Headings and lists:** use direct action headings for a recommendation or plan and
  bullets for parallel items. Keep explanations in connected prose; a short note may
  need neither headings nor a list.
- **Tables:** use a compact table when the reader compares the same attributes across
  items. Do not turn long explanations into cells merely to make them look structured.
- **Emojis:** use them sparingly when welcomed by the author and audience and when they
  help identify an action or status. Do not treat them as a default emphasis style.

Check rendered emphasis, list hierarchy and indentation in the destination. An independent
export check does not establish that another viewer renders the same file correctly.

## Choose the register

### Executive and business writing

Lead with the decision, risk, result, or request. Keep commercial and organizational vocabulary when it carries precision. Name owners and dates when supplied. Avoid consultant filler, ceremonial importance, and decorative frameworks.

When making a recommendation, use an actionable verb and a concrete object rather than
"Propose a change" when the recommendation is already being made. Name the object before
shortening it to "program," "record" or "field." Keep the reasoning needed to act; remove
alternative designs and explanatory caveats that do not change the reader's decision.
Preserve material uncertainty, qualifications and approval boundaries.

### Technical documentation

Optimize for correct action. Preserve identifiers and defined terms. Prefer actor-action sentences and concrete conditions. Repetition is often safer than synonym cycling. Do not add personality that competes with instructions.

#### Controlled English (opt-in)

Use this register when the user asks for controlled, plain or "STE" English, or when the reader must act on the text in a second language or through translation. It is based on [ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, Issue 9](https://www.asd-ste100.org/assets/files/ASD-STE100_ISSUE9.pdf), a standard written for aerospace maintenance documents. Apply these rules in full only when the user asks for strict compliance; otherwise apply them most of the way, the "80% of the way to ASD-STE100" that [Karpathy suggests](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2105819303471976479) for explanations.

- One instruction per sentence, unless the actions happen at the same time. Put instructions in the imperative.
- At most 20 words in a procedural sentence (this includes warnings and cautions) and 25 in a descriptive sentence. At most six sentences in a paragraph.
- Active voice. Use the passive only in description, and only when the actor is unknown.
- Simple present, past and future tenses. No progressive or perfect constructions.
- Use one word for one meaning throughout. Keep the user's technical names, identifiers and units exactly.
- Noun clusters of at most three words. Write a longer technical name in full once, then give it a short form.
- Keep articles and demonstratives ("the", "this"). Do not drop them to save words.

The standard also has a controlled dictionary of about 900 approved words. Do not claim conformance without checking against it. Do not apply this register to persuasive, narrative or voice-matched writing; there it removes the author's voice and makes the text read as flat as the generic prose this skill exists to fix.

### Email and chat

Match the relationship and power distance. Keep greetings and sign-offs that fit the sender. Use contractions if the author does. Do not turn a short note into a memo or add warmth the sender did not express.

### Essays, posts, and opinion

Preserve stance, tension, humor, digressions, and uneven pacing when they do real work. Specific observations beat staged candor and fake profundity. Do not sand down disagreement into neutral corporate prose.

### Marketing and product copy

Use customer evidence, mechanisms, outcomes, and constraints. A strong claim needs support. Do not replace every adjective; replace unsupported praise with proof. Keep intentional brand language when supplied.

### Scripts meant to be spoken

Talk tracks, demo scripts, presenter notes, video narration. The whole register, its patterns and its checks live in [spoken-register.md](spoken-register.md); read that file before any spoken pass. The one-line test: can a person say this on a stage at normal speed, and does the room follow it cold?

### Academic, legal, medical, and policy writing

Preserve qualifications, defined terms, citations, and domain conventions. Clarity does not authorize stronger certainty. Active voice is useful, not mandatory. Do not simplify away a distinction that governs interpretation.

## Resolve conflicts

Apply this order:

1. factual and legal integrity;
2. explicit user instructions;
3. channel and audience requirements;
4. reliable author samples;
5. the current draft's voice;
6. generic style guidance.

When two samples conflict, prefer the one closest to the target channel and date. When a sample's habit hurts comprehension, preserve its flavor but repair the local sentence.
