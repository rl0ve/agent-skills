# Voice and register

## Build a small voice model

Use representative samples from the same author and, when possible, the same channel. Infer tendencies across samples:

- sentence length and variation;
- paragraph density;
- common words and tolerated jargon;
- directness, warmth, skepticism, humor, and emotional range;
- first- and second-person use;
- punctuation, fragments, contractions, and parenthetical asides;
- how the writer opens, transitions, qualifies claims, and ends.

Prefer stable tendencies over a memorable one-off line. Match the author's range, not a caricature. Never reuse a distinctive phrase from an unrelated sample as if it belonged in the new subject.

If no sample exists, infer voice conservatively from the draft. Preserve unusual specifics, mixed feelings, and defensible quirks. Do not manufacture typos, slang, randomness, or false autobiography to seem human.

## Choose the register

### Executive and business writing

Lead with the decision, risk, result, or request. Keep commercial and organizational vocabulary when it carries precision. Name owners and dates when supplied. Avoid consultant filler, ceremonial importance, and decorative frameworks.

### Technical documentation

Optimize for correct action. Preserve identifiers and defined terms. Prefer actor-action sentences and concrete conditions. Repetition is often safer than synonym cycling. Do not add personality that competes with instructions.

### Email and chat

Match the relationship and power distance. Keep greetings and sign-offs that fit the sender. Use contractions if the author does. Do not turn a short note into a memo or add warmth the sender did not express.

### Essays, posts, and opinion

Preserve stance, tension, humor, digressions, and uneven pacing when they do real work. Specific observations beat staged candor and fake profundity. Do not sand down disagreement into neutral corporate prose.

### Marketing and product copy

Use customer evidence, mechanisms, outcomes, and constraints. A strong claim needs support. Do not replace every adjective; replace unsupported praise with proof. Keep intentional brand language when supplied.

### Scripts meant to be spoken

Talk tracks, demo scripts, presenter notes, video narration. The test for every sentence: can a person say this on a stage at normal speed, and does the room follow it cold?

- Gloss what the screen says; never read a label or column value as if it were a sentence. "No rule resolves a combined cause" is a cell, not speech - say what it means and who acts on it.
- Speak in the room, not about the talk. No section or act numbers aloud, no narrating the audience ("they are easy to mix up"), no stage directions in the words ("hold that pattern").
- Land callbacks forwards. "The ledger was empty when we published the map. It is not empty now" does the same work as "Remember the empty ledger?" without asking the room to go back and fetch it, and it survives a listener who missed the first mention. Each backwards reference looks harmless alone, so this is a set-level check: count "Remember…", "Keep that in mind", "hold that", "as you saw earlier", and anything pointing at the deck's own structure ("what the last act comes back to") across the whole script. More than one or two and it reads as a speaker who does not trust the material. A callback to a number the audience genuinely needs is fine; state it, do not request it.
- Every clause has to stand up on its own. A listener cannot look back, so a clause finished by an earlier one ("built for exactly that," "runs the way the map said it could") costs them the sentence they are currently in. Name the subject again rather than pointing at it, and define a thing by what it does rather than by what is absent.
- Vary sentence length inside the paragraph, and make the variety carry something. Three sentences of the same shape in a row read as a list being recited, and this is as true of short flat declaratives as of long hedged ones. Give one sentence a turn - a contrast, a repeat that lands, an ordinary phrase like "messy work" - and let its neighbours run longer or shorter.
- Round numbers out loud. Three or more exact figures in consecutive sentences cannot be heard, so keep the one the point rests on and approximate the rest audibly on purpose ("call it forty"). One hard constraint: never say an approximation that contradicts an exact number the audience can read on the screen behind you.
- Keep one name per thing, and keep it the name the screen shows. If the column says "reason," do not switch to "cause" mid-walk unless the screen does.
- Emphasis is navigation, not decoration: on a script of any length, bold the number the speaker must hit and one load-bearing phrase per beat - two to five words, at least one per paragraph. This is the one register where added bold is earned; do not strip it as decorative.
- Start a new line where the beat changes, so the speaker can find their place at a glance.

### Academic, legal, medical, and policy writing

Preserve qualifications, defined terms, citations, and domain conventions. Clarity does not authorize stronger certainty. Active voice is useful, not mandatory. Do not simplify away a distinction that governs interpretation.

## Resolve conflicts

Apply this order:

1. factual and legal integrity;
2. explicit user instructions;
3. channel and audience requirements;
4. reliable author samples;
5. the current draft's voice;
6. generic style guidance.

When two samples conflict, prefer the one closest to the target channel and date. When a sample's habit hurts comprehension, preserve its flavor but repair the local sentence.
