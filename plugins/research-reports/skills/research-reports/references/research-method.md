# Research method

## Find what could change the answer

Begin with a few questions tied to the reader's decision, not a source-collection
quota. State the leading explanation and credible alternatives. Seek the strongest
counterexample, relevant work already under way and evidence that the proposed remedy
could miss the actual constraint. Stop expanding the search when new sources no
longer materially change the conclusion; record a consequential unresolved gap.

For a continuation, inspect the actual original prompt and generated artifacts when
accessible. A shared excerpt, assistant recap or attachment title can omit later
steering or an entire deliverable. Preserve source IDs within their original namespace
or make an explicit mapping; never reuse an ID for a different source silently.

Match the investigation to the breadth of the question. For organizational strategy,
check the relevant primary strategy records and how distinct audiences, routes to
market and product roles relate. A deep product case can test a wider argument, but
does not establish company-wide coverage. Identify which parts are supported, sampled
or still unexamined; do not relabel a similarly themed meeting as the recalled one.

## Give each source the authority it earns

Use an exact source link and enough context to support the associated claim. A
primary transcript supports attribution differently from generated meeting notes.
A human discussion can establish a reported obstacle or decision, but not a population
rate. A roadmap shows intent, not delivery. Directly reading a dashboard verifies its
displayed content, not the underlying calculation or accepted customer value.

Follow secondary mentions to accessible originals when the claim depends on them.
Compare source and event dates, definitions, scope and later replies. An old issue is
not a current defect until its status or behavior is checked. A public vendor claim
is not an independent benchmark; a proposed operating practice is not an observed
performance gain. Do not treat several retellings of one result as independent proof.

Describe actual source access precisely: a fresh query, saved extract, displayed
dashboard, schema inspection and failed connection establish different things. Do not
imply every requested system was queried because related data appeared elsewhere.

Keep a bounded claim record, for example:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Claim and stable ID | Keeps the report, aid and visual consistent. |
| Source and evidence type | Distinguishes observed, reported, inferred and proposed. |
| Date/version and scope | Prevents a historical or partial fact becoming universal. |
| Population, unit, period | Prevents incompatible numbers becoming one funnel. |
| Counterevidence or gap | Makes the conclusion and its limits inspectable. |

For confidential work, keep source captures, queries, identifying details and the
ledger in the task's authorized workspace. Reusable methods and public skill packages
must not absorb the private research example.

## Quantitative depth and causal restraint

Choose measures by the job, not by whichever large number is easiest to obtain.
Separate reach, intent-qualified starts, correct completion, recurrence, work scope,
help required and accepted business outcome. A useful low-frequency process may not
produce daily visits; unattended work can recur without a person reopening the UI.

For measured claims establish definition, grain, population, window, freshness and
lineage. Keep parent accounts, subsidiaries, people, projects, primary transactions
and invoked child executions distinct. Preserve pending, failed, unknown and unmatched
records. A selected sample that favors established workloads cannot estimate all-user
adoption. Show plausible selection and missingness effects.

For persona or customer-origin claims, inspect populated values and coverage, not
just available field names. CRM contacts, product users, buyers and acquisition sources
are different populations. Check identity joins and overlapping groups before combining
them. Sparse role labels or account-level product overlap cannot establish the share
of developers, who introduced the product, or the route that caused adoption. Keep
unknowns visible rather than treating missing labels as a negative classification.

Do not infer acquisition from first product activity, retention from the returning
share of today's active users, or business value from runtime thresholds. Compare
growth only across compatible periods and definitions. A small before/after pilot
can establish task correctness or effort changes without establishing causal ARR,
renewal, savings or population-wide conversion lift.

## Make an actionable, independent synthesis

For the strongest findings, explain what changes in the decision and why. Separate
an addition to an existing strategy from an actual contradiction. Qualify novel claims
against what the reviewed stakeholders already said or planned; private knowledge
cannot be inferred from the accessible record.

Use a recommendation's full implementation and maintenance cost, dependencies and
falsifier. Surface what can stop or be retired, not only work to add. Estimates need
their assumptions and sensitivity; do not invent precision or turn a pilot ambition
into a forecast. Prefer an information-gathering step when modest uncertainty reverses
the decision.

Match the report's structure to the request. A useful decision report usually makes
the question, findings, evidence, alternatives, recommendation and limitations easy to
find; its sections and counts need not be identical across tasks.
