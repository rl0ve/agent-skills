A storm cancels four flights, and the recovery is finished before the gate agent is asked a single question.

At 7:14pm a line of thunderstorms closes the field. Four departures cancel, and 612 people who expected to sleep at home are now standing in a concourse. The old version of this evening is three teams working from three different screens, and a queue that stops moving.

This board follows one of those passengers, Dana, through the evening: the cancellation lands, seats are found on other flights, a crew is checked for legal duty hours and reassigned, and Dana gets a new boarding pass on her phone before she reaches the front of the line.

What's on screen, in order:

  1. SkyOps — the web console where the duty manager sees the disruption and runs the rebooking.
  2. CrewDesk — the desktop tool that checks duty-hour legality and reassigns crew.
  3. The passenger app — where Dana gets the new flight, the seat, and the meal voucher.

What happens, in plain words

A weather cell parks itself over the airport at 7:14pm. Four flights that were supposed to leave that evening will not leave. About 612 people are affected, and roughly a third of them are connecting to somewhere else, which means the problem is bigger than four flights.

The duty manager opens one screen and sees the whole shape of it: which flights, how many people, how many are connections, and how many seats exist on later flights tonight. She does not assemble that picture from four systems. It is the first thing she sees.

She starts the rebooking. The system proposes seats for everyone it can place, ranked by who is most stuck: unaccompanied minors and passengers with tight connections first, then everyone else. She reviews the exceptions rather than the whole list, because the ones that worked do not need her.

Rebooking passengers creates a second problem. Two of the later flights now need crew who were not scheduled to work them, and duty-hour rules are not negotiable. CrewDesk checks legality for every candidate and returns the ones who can actually fly, with the reason the others cannot.

Dana, meanwhile, has been standing in a line. Her phone buzzes. She has a seat on the 9:40, a gate, a meal voucher, and a hotel offer if she wants it. She leaves the line.

The recovery finishes at 8:02pm. The measure of success is not that a system did something clever. It is that the queue never formed.

Invented scenario and invented products, built to demonstrate the storyboard format. Any resemblance to a real airline system is coincidental.
